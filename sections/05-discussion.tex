\section{Discussion}%
\label{sec:discussion}

Our evaluation shows that
semantics-based test generalization via symbolic analysis
is viable but currently constrained to
specific application environments and test architectures.
Nevertheless, \ToolTeralizer{}'s applicability exceeds
what JARVIS --- the closest prior work --- reports:
\ToolTeralizer{} produces generalized tests in
\TzRqzeroCensusPopulatedProjects{} of the 12 benchmark projects of JARVIS's evaluation,
whereas JARVIS reports generalization success for only 2 of them (RQ0).
Under controlled conditions that match current capabilities,
\ToolTeralizer{} successfully generalized 40.1\% of assertions (RQ5)
and improved mutation detection by 0.8--3.9 percentage points (RQ1).
As post-processing for generated tests,
generalization offers competitive efficiency:
combining 1-second \ToolEvoSuite{} generation with \ToolTeralizer{}'s generalization
achieved comparable mutation detection to 60-second generation
while reducing processing time by 31.9\% (RQ4).
Similarly, keeping the generation budget unchanged
but investing additional runtime in generalization
extended the Pareto frontier to detection rates
that longer \ToolEvoSuite{}-only generation did not reach (RQ4).
Generalization of real-world projects faces several barriers (RQ6):
only \TzRealworldAssertionsIncludedPct{} of assertions remain included,
compared with 47.8\% under controlled conditions.
\TzRealworldApplicabilityProjects{} of \TzRealworldEligibleProjects{} eligible projects complete the
pipeline, and \TzRealworldGeneralizationsFinalUsable{} generalized tests complete all five stages,
including mutation testing. The first major project-level exclusions occur during project, test, and assertion analysis.
Later widening refusals show where the extracted path condition and symbolic output do not provide
enough evidence for a sound generalized oracle.
This section discusses when and why generalization succeeds (Section~\ref{sec:when-it-works}),
when and why it fails (Section~\ref{sec:when-it-fails}),
and how future work
can improve generalization effectiveness, efficiency, and applicability (Section~\ref{sec:how-to-improve}).

\subsection{When and Why Generalization Succeeds}%
\label{sec:when-it-works}

Within the evaluated projects,
generalization succeeds when implementation and test properties
of the target projects align
with \ToolTeralizer{}'s current static analysis capabilities
and \ToolSPF{}'s symbolic analysis capabilities.
Implementation code that is amenable to generalization
is primarily focused on numeric computations
in pure deterministic functions without side effects
(thus enabling symbolic analysis)
and uses standard project structures that facilitate detection
of required output artifacts such as compilation outputs
as well as (mutation) testing and coverage reports.
Tests amenable to generalization are single-assertion tests
without complex setup logic, loops, or interprocedural control flow.
% Together, these structural properties enable
% reliable static analysis for identifying tested methods
% and symbolic execution for extracting path conditions and output expressions.

When these conditions are satisfied,
\ToolTeralizer{} achieves moderate mutation score improvements at reasonable runtime cost.
Mutation scores increased by
\TzEffectivenessEqbenchEvosuiteMutationImprovementMinPp{}--\TzEffectivenessEqbenchEvosuiteMutationImprovementMaxPp{}
percentage points in \DatasetsEqBenchEs{}
and by \TzEffectivenessCommonsEvosuiteMutationImprovementMinPp{}--\TzEffectivenessCommonsEvosuiteMutationImprovementMaxPp{}
percentage points in \DatasetsCommonsEs{}
compared to \ToolEvoSuite{}-generated baselines (Figure~\ref{fig:mutation-detection-results}).
These gains are bounded by construction.
A generalized test executes the MUT call of its assertion
with inputs from the input partition of the original execution path.
It can therefore detect only mutants that these inputs execute,
and it raises the mutation score only for mutants
that the \VariantInitial{} test suite does not already detect.
Mutants of methods or paths that no generalizable assertion covers are out of reach.
Beyond effectiveness, generalization offers competitive efficiency when combined with test generation.
% where \ToolEvoSuite{} achieves breadth through coverage-guided exploration
% and \ToolTeralizer{} achieves depth through partition-based input generation within already-covered test execution paths.
For example, 1-second \ToolEvoSuite{} generation with \VariantNaiveB{} generalization
achieves a 51.7\% mutation detection rate in 37,533~seconds,
compared to 51.6\% in 55,075~seconds for 60-second generation alone
(Figure~\ref{fig:teralizer-efficiency}).
Investing additional runtime in generalization increases the mutation detection rate further,
e.g., to 53.8\% in 48,269~seconds for 10-second \ToolEvoSuite{} generation with \VariantImprovedC{} generalization.

Outcomes vary based on constraint complexity and
original test suite effectiveness of the target projects.
More complex constraints hinder mutation score improvements
because they increase the number of \texttt{Too\-Many\-Filter\-Misses\-Exceptions}.
Similarly, stronger original test suites leave less room for mutation score improvements.
For example, \VariantNaive{} and \VariantImproved{}
both show larger mutation score improvements
for \DatasetsEqBenchEs{} than for \DatasetsCommonsEs{}
because of the simpler constraints in \DatasetsEqBenchEs{}
(137--231 vs. 290--507 average operation counts, Table~\ref{tab:mutation-detection-comparison}),
and larger mutation score improvements for \DatasetsCommonsEs{} than for \DatasetCommonsDev{}
because of \DatasetCommonsDev{}'s stronger original tests
(56.77--58.12\% vs. \TzEffectivenessCommonsDeveloperBaselineMutationScorePct{}
\VariantInitial{} mutation detection rate, Figure~\ref{fig:mutation-detection-results}).

\VariantNaive{} is more effective than \VariantImproved{} for simpler constraints
(Figure~\ref{fig:mutation-detection-results}, rows 1--3),
whereas \VariantImproved{} is more effective for more complex constraints
(Figure~\ref{fig:mutation-detection-results}, rows 4--6).
Two factors explain these results (Section~\ref{sec:constraint-complexity-eval}).
First, simpler constraints are easier to satisfy by chance.
Consequently, the difference in \texttt{Too\-Many\-Filter\-Misses\-Exceptions}
between \VariantNaive{} and \VariantImproved{} is smaller in such cases
than for more complex constraints.
Second, simpler constraints enable \VariantImproved{}
to more reliably encode input partition boundaries.
As a result, it spends more \tries{} on boundary testing
but neglects non-boundary testing,
limiting mutation detection improvements.
This is more pronounced at low \tries{} settings
where \VariantImprovedA{} underperforms
all other generalization strategies
(Figure~\ref{fig:mutation-detection-results}, rows 1--3).

\subsection{When and Why Generalization Fails}%
\label{sec:when-it-fails}

RQ6 shows that generalization is unsuccessful for many evaluated real-world projects
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval-extended}).
Broadly speaking, there are three underlying causes that are responsible for the majority of exclusions.
Prototype and project-integration limitations restrict the tests and projects that
\ToolTeralizer{} can analyze.
Tested method identification and specification extraction
can fail to provide the necessary evidence
required to create a sound generalized PBT\@.
Build, execution, and environment failures can stop processing
even when the remaining generalization mechanism is applicable.
The following discussion interprets their effects
and motivates potential directions for future improvements
in Section~\ref{sec:how-to-improve}.

\paragraph{Implementation Limitations}%
\label{par:implementation-limitations}

Four implementation limitations directly constrain what the current prototype can achieve.
First, \ToolTeralizer{} only supports standard \texttt{@Test} methods from JUnit~4 and 5,
JUnit~3 tests that inherit from \texttt{TestCase}, and the six assertion types listed in
Section~\ref{sec:test-assertion-analysis}.
Second, \ToolTeralizer{} generalizes a test only when it finds an assertion in the test method.
Third, its intraprocedural assertion detection does not follow calls to helper methods.
Fourth, its build integration expects compilation outputs and test reports in the default Maven or Gradle build directories.

Among these limitations, unsupported types of tests (e.g., \texttt{@Theory})
have a comparatively small impact on the results:
the \texttt{TestType} filter rejects 0.2\% of tests that reach it
(Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-filtering-extended}).
Assertion recognition is more consequential, as \texttt{NoAssertions} rejects 32.9\% of tests that reach it.
A stratified sample estimates that only
\TzRqsixReconstructionNoAssertionsGenuineAbsenceEstimatePct{} of these rejected tests genuinely
contain no assertion (95\% CI\@:
\TzRqsixReconstructionNoAssertionsGenuineAbsenceCiLowerPct{}--
\TzRqsixReconstructionNoAssertionsGenuineAbsenceCiUpperPct{}).
Most rejections therefore indicate assertions
that the current intraprocedural analysis does not recognize.
These test-level exclusions also prevent later tested method identification and specification
extraction, so they can shadow downstream limitations.

\paragraph{Specification Extraction Challenges}%
\label{par:specification-extraction-challenges}

Tested method identification supplies the MUT-to-assertion mapping required for specification
extraction.
\ToolTeralizer{} traces the value observed by an assertion through its data dependencies to the call
that produced it.
When this trace does not uniquely identify one call as the MUT,
\ToolTeralizer{} selects one of the plausible MUT candidates
based on a ranking heuristic (Section~\ref{sec:tested-method-identification}).
The \texttt{MissingValue} filter rejects 39.1\% of real-world assertions when the analysis cannot
identify a MUT at all (Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-filtering-extended}).
Potential causes of such exclusions are unsupported assertions,
unresolved MUT declarations, and assertions
that simply are not connected to any visible method call.
A manual review of 70 heuristic selections found \TzRqsixReconstructionAssertionToMutReviewedSupportedMapping{}
supported and \TzRqsixReconstructionAssertionToMutReviewedContradictedMapping{} contradicted mappings.
Thus, the review confirms that the ranking heuristic can select an incorrect MUT\@.
However, all incorrect selections were later excluded by downstream filters.

After a MUT is identified, input and output types and symbolic evidence
constrain the extractable specification.
\texttt{ParameterType} rejects 36.5\% of real-world assertions, while \texttt{ReturnType} rejects
\TzRealworldReturnTypeRejectedPct{} of assertions with recorded return type information
(Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-filtering-extended}).
These filters expose limited support for objects, collections, arrays, and other values that
\ToolSPF{}'s symbolic models cannot represent soundly.
During generalized test creation, the widening gate refuses another \TzRealworldWideningRefusals{} of
\TzRealworldGeneralizationAttempts{} generalization attempts (\TzRealworldWideningRefusalsPct{}).
A refusal occurs when the extracted path condition and symbolic output do not establish
that the original oracle remains valid for new inputs.
Thus, broader applicability requires stronger models or evidence rather than weaker acceptance criteria.

\paragraph{Dependencies and Environment}%
\label{par:environmental-issues}

Build failures, failing original tests, external-tool failures, and resource limits
reduce end-to-end applicability for reasons distinct from
implementation and specification extraction limitations
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval-extended} and Table~\ref{tab:processing-failures}).
For example, among \TzRealworldJpfUncaughtExceptionDiagnostics{} uncaught exceptions
recorded during \ToolSPF{} analysis, post-run classification
attributes \TzRealworldJpfUncaughtExceptionReclassified{}
(\TzRealworldJpfUncaughtExceptionReclassifiedPct{}) to application exceptions,
missing native peers, or incomplete \ToolSPF{} models and fields.
These failures require changes to projects, tool integration, resource limits, or symbolic models
rather than improvements to the generalization approach itself.
Project integration assumptions create a narrower limitation.
A manual review of \TzRqsixReconstructionOutputDirectoriesTotal{} preserved output-directory records
identifies \TzRqsixReconstructionOutputDirectoriesDefaultDirectoryMismatch{} default-directory
mismatch. The other records contain absent project artifacts such as required databases
or unreachable external services.

\subsection{Directions for Future Improvements}%
\label{sec:how-to-improve}

Based on our findings, three improvement directions emerge for semantics-based test generalization:
(i)~expanding applicability to support a broader range of projects, tests, and assertions,
(ii)~improving effectiveness when generalization does apply,
and (iii)~improving efficiency in terms of generalization runtime
as well as size and runtime of generalized test suites.
This section discusses opportunities in each direction,
distinguishing engineering improvements
achievable through implementation effort
from research challenges requiring advances in underlying techniques.

\subsubsection{Improving Applicability}

The primary barrier to real-world adoption is limited applicability:
only \TzRealworldAssertionsIncludedPct{} of real-world assertions reach generalized test creation (Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-breakdown-extended}),
compared with 47.8\% under controlled conditions (Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-breakdown}).
Three categories of improvements could expand
the subset of projects, tests, and assertions amenable to generalization.

\textit{Type Support.}
Type limitations cause a large portion of assertion-level exclusions.
For example, the \texttt{ReturnType} filter rejects \TzRealworldReturnTypeRejectedPct{}
of assertions with recorded return type information
in the evaluated RepoReapers projects (Table~\ref{tab:exclusions-filtering-extended}).
The \texttt{ParameterType} filter similarly rejects many assertions because their
selected MUT has no parameter with a supported input type.
Expanding symbolic type support remains a fundamental research
challenge~\cite{amadini_2021_string_survey,zhong_2021_arrays,chen_2024_z3noodler,chocholaty_2025_z3noodler,sun_2024_cgs}:
precise constraint modeling for strings, arrays, and objects
requires advances in symbolic analysis
that go beyond the capabilities of current tools and approaches.
On the input side, however, property-based testing is already an incomplete, sampling-based approach~\cite{claessen_2000_quickcheck}:
a generator that samples valid inputs from a subset of the partition only narrows which inputs are tested without risking spurious test failures.
Grammar inference and learned input generators produce such samplers without a complete formal model~\cite{bastani_2017_glade,gopinath_2020_mimid,zhang_2025_g2fuzz}.
For complex objects that the symbolic execution engine cannot construct directly, \citeauthor{thummalapenta_2011_retrofitting}~\cite{thummalapenta_2011_retrofitting} manually created factory methods whose method-call sequences build the required object states, and recent work automates parts of this synthesis through static call-chain analysis~\cite{yang_2026_telpa} or LLM-generated input and object builders~\cite{tu_2026_cottontail}.
Furthermore, type limitations shadow other issues.
Thus, as type support improves,
additional limitations in assertion-to-MUT mapping and general assertion support
would become visible, enabling more in-depth analysis and targeted improvement of these causes.

\textit{Specification Extraction.}
At the widening gate, \ToolTeralizer{} refuses \TzRealworldWideningRefusalsPct{} of real-world generalization attempts when single-path analysis returns a concrete output value because data flow crosses unmerged local branches or input-dependent loops (Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval-extended}).
Conversely, when extraction succeeds on paths with complex control flow or many loop iterations, the resulting symbolic expressions can exceed \ToolTeralizer{}'s specification size limit (Listing~\ref{lst:jpf-config} and Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval}).
Bounded path-region merging~\cite{sharma_2020_ranger} and loop summarization~\cite{godefroid_2011_automatic,xie_2016_proteus,kapus_2019_loops} can fold multi-branch assignments and linear induction variables into compact closed-form expressions.
Where exact closed-form output formulas cannot be extracted or grow too large, a test generalizer could instead weaken the oracle from exact output equality to partial or relational postconditions, such as the interval bounds, ordering constraints, and linear relationships inferred by abstract interpretation~\cite{cousot_1977_abstract,partush_2014_abstract} and specification inference techniques~\cite{ernst_2007_daikon,flanagan_2001_houdini,padhi_2016_pie} (Section~\ref{sec:rw-inference}).
Recent work also explores using LLMs to synthesize loop invariants, postconditions, or property-based tests directly~\cite{kamath_2024_loopy,ma_2025_specgen,vikram_2024_pbt,li_2026_probe}.
However, unverified LLM-generated properties frequently fail on correct implementations~\cite{vikram_2024_pbt}, whereas sound LLM specification synthesizers rely on iterative verifier and solver calls~\cite{kamath_2024_loopy,ma_2025_specgen}, so hybrid approaches must balance specification recovery against solver cost and the soundness of the resulting oracle.

\textit{Static Analysis.}
The evaluation results identify several static analysis limitations.
Interprocedural analysis could recognize assertions delegated to helper methods.
Support for AssertJ, Truth, and Hamcrest matchers beyond equality could recognize additional oracles.
Improved MUT identification could add support for further cases.
Tests that genuinely lack assertions could be modeled as implicit ``does not throw'' checks,
thus eliminating the need to exclude tests without assertions
due to a lack of validated oracles.
TestNG and JUnit theory support would
address their corresponding \texttt{TestType} rejections.
Furthermore, handling of inherited tests could be improved
to capture yet another subset of cases.

\subsubsection{Improving Effectiveness}

When generalization does apply, effectiveness depends on
the generation of inputs that thoroughly cover the valid input space.
Two factors influence this:
(i)~the generation strategy,
which determines whether inputs are sampled randomly (\VariantNaive{})
or specifically target input partition boundaries (\VariantImproved{}),
and (ii)~constraint encoding,
which determines how much of the extracted specification can be used to guide generation.

\textit{Generation Strategies.}
Constraint-aware input generation used by \VariantImproved{}
increases detection rate improvements of boundary-related mutations
such as \texttt{ConditionalsBoundary} compared to \VariantNaive{} (+2.55pp vs.\ +1.21pp,
Section~\ref{sec:primary-effects-eval}).
However, focusing too much on boundaries limits arithmetic diversity
within available \tries{}.
This negatively affects detection rate improvements of \texttt{Math} mutations,
particularly at lower \tries{} settings (Section~\ref{sec:constraint-complexity-eval}).
To improve detection rates without increasing \tries{},
more balanced generation strategies can be developed
that use heuristics based on constraint complexity or other source code properties
to better balance boundary vs.\ non-boundary testing,
thus utilizing the benefits of both random and boundary-focused
generation where each provides the largest benefit.

\textit{Constraint Encoding.}
Average constraint utilization per project ranges from 10\% to 69\%
in controlled settings
(Table~\ref{tab:mutation-detection-comparison}).
This is because \ToolTeralizer{}'s \VariantImproved{} generalization strategy
only encodes simple in-/equalities on variables and constants,
whereas compound terms such as \texttt{a == (b + 1)}
are enforced through filtering (Section~\ref{sec:generalized-test-creation}).
Extending constraint encoding support would enable more precise boundary testing
while reducing \texttt{TooManyFilterMissesException}s
that affect 5.7--15.7\% of generalizations across datasets
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval}).
However, as constraint complexity increases,
generating valid inputs becomes increasingly difficult.
Techniques such as SMT-based constraint solving~\cite{de_moura_2008_z3,ringer_2017_iorek},
targeted property-based testing~\cite{loscher_2017_targeted},
or coverage-guided property-based testing~\cite{lampropoulos_2019_fuzzchick}
could more effectively generate inputs satisfying complex constraints
than \ToolJqwik{}'s primarily random generation,
albeit at increased computational cost.

\subsubsection{Improving Efficiency}

Efficiency improvements could be implemented along the following three dimensions:
(i)~tool runtime, which determines processing cost during generalization,
(ii)~test suite runtime, which determines execution cost after generalization,
and (iii)~test suite size, which primarily affects maintenance overhead.

\textit{Tool Runtime.}
Processing costs are dominated by mutation testing,
which consumes 59.2--95.7\% of total pipeline runtime
(Figure~\ref{fig:teralizer-runtimes}).
However, not all mutation operators benefit equally from generalization
(Table~\ref{tab:detections-per-mutator}).
Focusing on operators that benefit the most from generalization
and using lightweight heuristics based on constraint complexity
or assertion patterns to identify unlikely-beneficial candidates before mutation testing
could reduce processing time at the cost of potentially missing some improvements.
Incremental processing that targets only newly-added or modified tests
would avoid repeated analysis of stable code in continuous integration settings.
Arcmutate~\cite{arcmutate_2024}, a commercial extension of \ToolPit{},
offers an accelerator plugin that could further reduce processing costs.

\textit{Test Suite Runtime.}
Test suite runtime increases caused by generalization stem primarily from \ToolJqwik{} framework overhead
(approximately 150ms per test, Figure~\ref{fig:test-runtime-differences})
and filter-and-regenerate cycles when inputs violate constraints.
\VariantImproved{} reduces filter miss rates by 46.8--58.4\%
compared to \VariantNaive{} (Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval}),
which reduces execution cost per successfully generated test input from 28.61ms to 18.92ms at 10~\tries{}
and from 5.68ms to 1.98ms at 200~\tries{} (Figure~\ref{fig:test-runtime-differences}).
Better constraint encoding would further reduce filter-and-regenerate cycles
by enabling more inputs to be generated directly rather than requiring filtering.
Additionally, \ToolJqwik{}~2 plans parallelization support~\cite{link_2024_jqwik2},
which could reduce test suite execution time
by distributing property-based test execution across multiple cores.

\textit{Test Suite Size.}
Our evaluation shows observed LOC increases of 4.9--58.7\% across projects
(Table~\ref{tab:lines-per-project}).
These increases can be attributed to two primary causes:
explicit constraint encoding in generalized tests
and structural duplication from test isolation
(Section~\ref{sec:ancillary-effects-eval}).
Abstracting constraint encoding in a library
could reduce this overhead,
and tighter integration of generalized tests into original test classes
would avoid duplication from copied imports and helper methods.
Test suite reduction could also be extended
to replace multiple original tests that cover the same partition
with a single property-based test,
thus reducing test count instead of only compensating for added tests.
This mirrors the idea of test suite reduction via
parameterization~\cite{tsukamoto_2018_autoput,azamnouri_2021_compressing},
but would use semantics-based analysis rather than syntactic clone detection
to identify the original tests that can be merged.

\subsubsection{Deployment Strategies}

Our results position semantics-based test generalization
for targeted deployment during new unit test development
or as a post-processing step for generated tests in numeric-heavy domains.
During new unit test development,
developers could write tests with generalization in mind.
A developer writes one test per execution path with a typical input,
and generalization extends it to the boundaries of that path's input partition,
which \VariantImproved{} targets explicitly (Section~\ref{sec:constraint-encoding}).
\VariantImprovedC{} gains about twice as much as \VariantNaiveC{} on boundary mutations
(\texttt{ConditionalsBoundary} in Table~\ref{tab:detections-per-mutator}),
so this strategy directs generalization at the inputs a typical-case test is most likely to miss.
Other input selection strategies within a partition remain future work,
and our evaluation does not cover test suites written for this purpose.
Because generalization success depends not only on domain characteristics
but also on program and test architecture,
developers adopting automated test generalization tools
such as \ToolTeralizer{} can improve generalization outcomes
through implementation choices independent of further tool advances:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Following a functional programming style that emphasizes pure functions
    reduces assertion-to-MUT mapping failures.
  \item Placing assertions directly in test methods rather than delegating to helper methods
    reduces assertion detection failures due to the current intraprocedural analysis.
  \item Favoring supported assertions such as \texttt{assertEquals}
    over unsupported ones such as \texttt{assertThat} where this is feasible
    reduces assertion type exclusions.
  \item Maintaining a green original test suite by addressing flaky tests and missing dependencies
    avoids exclusions from failing tests.
  \item Using standard project layouts and build output locations for test reports and coverage data
    reduces output detection failures.
\end{enumerate}

Beyond these factors,
test smells~\cite{garousi_2018_test_smells,van_deursen_2001_refactoring,meszaros_2007_xunit}
represent another factor that affects generalizability.
For example, \emph{Eager Test}
(where tests invoke multiple production methods)
complicates assertion-to-MUT mapping
(Section~\ref{sec:tested-method-identification})
and \emph{Conditional Test Logic}
(where tests contain loops or other conditionals)
can lead to inaccurate specifications
due to \ToolTeralizer{}'s limited loop handling
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval}).
Thus, another direction for future work
is developing automated transformation approaches
that refactor test code to improve generalizability
before applying tools like \ToolTeralizer{}.
Such transformations could build on
testability transformation techniques~\cite{harman_2004_testability},
which modify programs to improve test generation,
and recent advances in automated test smell detection~\cite{peruma_2020_tsdetect}
and refactoring~\cite{martins_2024_catalog,xuan_2016_brefactoring}.

\subsection{Threats to Validity}%
\label{sec:threats-to-validity}

\textit{Construct Validity.}
We use mutation score as a proxy for fault detection capability.
Fundamentally, this use of mutation testing
rests on two underlying hypotheses:
the competent programmer hypothesis,
which suggests that real faults are often
only small deviations from correct programs,
and the coupling effect,
which suggests that tests which are capable of detecting simple faults
will generally also detect more complex ones~\cite{demillo_1978_hints}.
Empirical studies validated the coupling effect~\cite{offutt_1992_coupling},
and subsequent work demonstrated that mutation scores correlate
with real fault detection~\cite{just_2014_mutants,papadakis_2018_correlation}.
Furthermore, surveys confirm the use
of mutation testing as a standard evaluation technique
in software testing research~\cite{jia_2011_analysis,papadakis_2019_mutation}.
%
While our use of \ToolPit{}'s \texttt{DEFAULTS} set of mutation operators
may not represent all fault types,
\texttt{DEFAULTS} is explicitly recommended by
\ToolPit{} as a stable set of operators
that minimizes equivalent mutants and avoids subsumption~\cite{coles_2021_less_is_more, coles_pit_mutators}.
%
Evaluating fault detection via mutation testing on suites that pass on the original version follows established practice in test amplification and oracle assessment~\cite{danglot_2019_dspot,tasnim_2026_togbench}.
Evaluating on real-world regressions would complement this setup, but existing benchmarks do not fit the current prototype's scope.
Real-world bug and regression datasets~\cite{just_2014_defects4j,saha_2018_bugsjar,madeiral_2019_bears,song_2022_regminer,silva_2024_gitbug} draw from general open source projects, where most tests and methods are excluded by the applicability barriers identified in RQ6 (Section~\ref{sec:limitations-eval-extended}).
Conversely, oracle and seeded-fault benchmarks~\cite{lin_2017_quixbugs,tasnim_2026_togbench} target single program versions rather than historical regressions, while symbolic analysis benchmarks~\cite{badihi_2021_eqbench,beyer_2026_svcomp} lack developer-written unit test suites.
Broadening tool applicability (Section~\ref{sec:how-to-improve}) would allow future evaluations to use established regression datasets directly.
However, because fundamental symbolic analysis limits will remain (Section~\ref{sec:symbolic-analysis}), future work could also curate regression benchmarks for supported program fragments as a fallback.

Mutation testing does not influence how generalized tests are created (Stages 1--4).
Stage~5 runs it once on the complete suite of original and generalized tests,
and the mutation scores reported for RQ1 and RQ2 are measured in that run,
before any test is removed.
The generalized oracle records the behavior of the analyzed implementation,
as do the assertions that \ToolEvoSuite{} infers.
For a developer-written original test,
the generalized test agrees with the developer's expected value at the original inputs.
For the remaining partition inputs,
the expected values come from the symbolic output expression.
The generalized test therefore cannot detect a fault
present when the specification was extracted
and that the original assertion did not expose.
This follows from the purpose of generalization:
the oracle characterizes the analyzed version,
and the generalized test detects later deviations from it (Section~\ref{sec:approach-concepts}).
Beyond the original inputs,
neither a generalized test nor an inferred assertion
states expected behavior independently of the implementation.

\textit{Internal Validity.}
Our experiments use single runs per configuration
with fixed random seeds for both \ToolEvoSuite{} and \ToolJqwik{}.
Results for individual classes, tests, or configurations might therefore differ for other seeds.
However, our findings are based on a large number of classes and tests
(Table~\ref{tab:dataset-statistics}),
and we observe consistent effectiveness and efficiency trends across projects
and configuration settings.
We therefore expect the overall results to be representative.
Additional runs would further increase their robustness.
Similarly, evaluation of higher \tries{}
and longer timeouts could provide further evidence of scaling behaviors.
%
The comparison with JARVIS (RQ0) relies on the results reported
in its paper rather than a full re-evaluation run.
This is because the JARVIS implementation is not publicly available.
We evaluate \ToolTeralizer{} on a best-effort reconstruction
of benchmark projects whose exact revisions are not specified by JARVIS\@.
The reconstructed snapshots may therefore differ
from the code on which the reported JARVIS results were produced.

\textit{External Validity.}
Our implementation targets Java~5--8 projects with JUnit~3--5 test suites
and build environments that use either Maven or Gradle as a build tool.
While the core approach is programming language-agnostic and could be
implemented for other languages and ecosystems
(e.g., using KLEE~\cite{cadar_2008_klee}
with RapidCheck~\cite{rapidcheck_2025} for~C/C++,
or CrossHair~\cite{crosshair_2025}
with Hypothesis~\cite{maciver_2019_hypothesis} for Python),
observed results might differ
due to language differences
and maturity of available tools.
%
Our evaluation of benefits
emphasizes projects that match current
symbolic analysis capabilities,
particularly regarding type support limitations.
As type support improves,
applicability of semantics-based test generalization
would directly benefit,
but results that can be achieved for non-numeric types might
differ from those we observed during generalization
of primarily numerical programs.
